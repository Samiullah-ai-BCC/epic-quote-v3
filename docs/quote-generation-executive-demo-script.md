# Epic Craftings Quote Generator — Executive Demo Script

**Target duration:** 6–7 minutes  
**Format:** Screen recording with AI voice-over and concise on-screen captions  
**Scope:** Authentication, quote generation, quote editing, persistence, and export  
**Suggested voice:** Conversational, confident, and professional without sounding like an advertisement

## Recording preparation

Before recording, prepare:

- A fictional customer with a clean company name, address, and contact
- A valid unused proposal ID
- One attractive, high-resolution artwork file
- One sign configuration that demonstrates mounting, dimensions, side views, and colors
- A second, throwaway quote on a **Flat Cut Acrylic/PVC Letters** type, open in another tab, purely
  for the 5-second locked-depth insert in Scene 3 (the channel-letter types carry no thickness, so
  the lock cannot be shown on the main sign)
- A short special requirement
- One additional line item and one discount to enter during editing
- An authenticator app with the demonstration account already enrolled
- A completed test run so loading delays and unexpected data do not interrupt the recording

Record at 1080p with the browser at approximately 90–100% zoom. Hide bookmarks, personal browser
extensions, notifications, passwords, recovery codes, customer information, and production secrets.

## Scene 1 — Secure authentication

**Approximate time:** 0:00–0:30

**Screen actions:**

1. Begin on the login page.
2. Enter the demonstration account credentials.
3. Show the mandatory authenticator-code screen.
4. Enter a current code and continue to All Quotes.

**Voice-over:**

> I’ll begin by signing into the Epic Craftings Quote Generator.
>
> Every user signs in with their own company account, and a password by itself is not enough to
> open the platform. A current code from the user’s authenticator is required as the second step.
>
> Once authentication is complete, we arrive at All Quotes, where the estimator can continue an
> existing job or begin a new one.

**On-screen captions:**

> Secure individual access  
> Mandatory two-factor authentication

**Recording note:** Do not show the QR enrolment secret, recovery codes, real passwords, or an
authenticator notification containing other account information.

## Scene 2 — Creating a new quote

**Approximate time:** 0:30–1:15

**Screen actions:**

1. Click **New Quote**.
2. Enter the proposal ID.
3. Search for and select the prepared company.
4. Show the address autofill and select the appropriate contact.
5. Enter the job name and quote source.
6. Add the prepared customer drawing or reference file.

**Voice-over:**

> I’ll create a new quote from here.
>
> First, we enter the proposal ID. This remains manual because it needs to follow the numbering
> system the team already uses.
>
> Then we select the customer company. If the company already exists in our data, the platform
> suggests it and brings in the saved address. We can also choose the relevant customer contact
> instead of retyping the same details on every quote.
>
> Next, we add the job name, where the opportunity came from, and any customer drawing or reference
> file we received.
>
> Nothing particularly dramatic at this stage—and that’s intentional. Quote intake should be
> quick, familiar, and predictable.

**On-screen captions:**

> Existing company information reused  
> Customer files stay with the quote

**Recording note:** the automatic sales-representative assignment is deliberately not shown. It
fills in only for Rod's and Ed's own accounts, so on the recording account the field stays blank
and the claim would not match the screen.

## Scene 3 — Selecting the sign configuration

**Approximate time:** 1:15–2:15

**Screen actions:**

1. Continue into the specification workflow.
2. Choose the prepared sign category and sign type.
3. Select a mounting option.
4. Enter height and width, and show the trim cap option appearing for this sign type.
5. Cut to a 5-second insert on **Flat Cut Acrylic/PVC Letters**: the depth box is filled from the
   standard data, greyed, and refuses to be typed in. Hover it so the tooltip is readable.
6. Cut back to the face-lit sign.
7. Select one or two meaningful construction or finish options.
8. Briefly show the generated specification text and item description.

**Voice-over:**

> Once the customer information is saved, we move into the actual sign configuration.
>
> For this example, I’m selecting a face-lit channel letter sign.
>
> From here, I can choose the mounting style, dimensions, finishes, and the construction options
> that are relevant to this particular sign type.
>
> The questions are contextual. In other words, the estimator only sees the options that make sense
> for the selected product. This sign type is asked for its trim cap; a type that doesn’t use one
> is never asked.
>
> The same principle protects standard construction values. On the flat-cut letter types, the
> material thickness comes from our own product data—so the depth is filled in for the estimator
> and can’t be typed over. Project-specific measurements stay fully editable.
>
> As these values are entered, the platform constructs the customer-facing specifications and item
> description automatically. We’re not asking the estimator to type the same technical description
> several times in different places.

**On-screen captions:**

> Contextual product questions  
> Standard thickness protected  
> Specifications generated automatically

---

## Scene 4 — Adding and preparing artwork

**Approximate time:** 2:15–2:55

**Screen actions:**

1. Upload the prepared artwork.
2. Open the crop interface.
3. Remove unnecessary whitespace and confirm the crop.
4. Move and resize the artwork in its available proposal area.

**Voice-over:**

> Next, we add the customer’s artwork.
>
> The uploaded file can be cropped directly inside the platform. We can remove unnecessary
> whitespace, keep the important part of the design, and confirm the crop without leaving the quote.
>
> Once it reaches the proposal, the artwork can be resized and repositioned within the available
> artwork area. The default fit uses most of the available height, but the estimator still has
> control when the layout needs a little adjustment.
>
> And, honestly, this is one of those small workflow improvements that saves more time than it
> initially appears to.

**On-screen caption:**

> Upload → Crop → Position

## Scene 5 — Reviewing the generated proposal

**Approximate time:** 2:55–3:55

**Screen actions:**

1. Open the proposal preview.
2. Move slowly across the customer information, item description, artwork, dimensions, side view,
   specifications, swatches, notes, quantity, and price.
3. Pause briefly on the side view and its related specifications.

**Voice-over:**

> Now we arrive at the proposal preview.
>
> The customer details and proposal information appear at the top. Under that, we have the item
> description, quantity, pricing, artwork, dimensions, specifications, construction side view,
> color references, and any additional requirements.
>
> The important thing here is that these sections aren’t independent pieces of text placed on a
> page. They’re connected to the information we entered in the workflow.
>
> The side view, for example, follows the selected sign configuration. If the sign type or mounting
> changes, the relevant construction diagram changes with it.
>
> The color swatches are connected to their corresponding specification lines. If the specification
> content moves, the swatches move with it instead of remaining stranded at an old position—unless
> the estimator has deliberately placed one somewhere, in which case it stays exactly where they
> put it.
>
> Special production instructions can also be separated from the main technical specifications and
> placed into the Special Requirements or Additional Notes section, where they’re much easier to
> notice.

**On-screen captions:**

> One connected source of information  
> Dynamic construction side views  
> Color references stay aligned

## Scene 6 — Editing the sign configuration and artwork

**Approximate time:** 3:55–4:50

**Screen actions:**

1. Open **Edit Specifications**.
2. Change one meaningful option, such as mounting or sign type.
3. Save and show the updated specifications, item description, and side view.
4. Reopen the artwork editor.
5. Make a small crop or positioning change and return directly to the preview.

**Voice-over:**

> Of course, quotes rarely remain exactly as they were first entered. Customers change dimensions,
> request another mounting method, replace artwork—or simply change their minds.
>
> So the proposal is designed to remain editable.
>
> I’ll change the mounting option in this example. When I save the change, the specifications update,
> the item description follows the new configuration, and the side view is refreshed where
> necessary.
>
> That’s the behaviour we want: one change should update the connected parts of the quote without
> forcing the estimator to rebuild everything manually.
>
> We can also return directly to the artwork editor, replace or crop the artwork again, and then
> come straight back to the preview.
>
> Existing positioning is preserved where it still belongs. At the same time, a genuinely new image
> can start with a fresh layout instead of inheriting an unrelated crop from the previous artwork.
> It’s a small distinction—but it prevents some very confusing results.

**On-screen caption:**

> One change updates the connected proposal

## Scene 7 — Proposal-level adjustments

**Approximate time:** 4:50–5:35

**Screen actions:**

1. Add one extra line item.
2. Add one discount.
3. Edit Additional Notes.
4. Customize or delete one color swatch.
5. Reposition and resize the side view.

**Voice-over:**

> There are also adjustments that belong to the individual proposal rather than the standard sign
> configuration.
>
> We can add an extra line item, apply a discount, update the additional notes, reposition a side
> view, or remove a color swatch that isn’t required for this particular job.
>
> Color labels can be customized, and the text reduces in size when necessary so it remains inside
> the swatch boundary.
>
> The goal isn’t to remove human control. It’s to automate the repetitive structure while still
> allowing the estimator to handle the unusual details that real projects always bring with them.

**On-screen caption:**

> Standardized where possible—flexible where necessary

**Approximate time:** 5:35–6:00

**Screen actions:**

1. Duplicate the current sign page.
2. Briefly show page reordering.
3. Add a blank page or customer document sheet.
4. Return to the primary sign page.

**Voice-over:**

> For larger opportunities, a quote can contain more than one sign.
>
> We can add another sign page, duplicate an existing page as a starting point, or change the order
> of the pages. Blank sheets and customer documents can also be included when the proposal needs
> supporting material.
>
> Each sign keeps its own artwork, specifications, side view, and layout, but the quote still exports
> as one coordinated document.
>
> I won’t configure the entire second sign here, otherwise this becomes a training video rather
> quickly. But the workflow is the same.

## Scene 9 — Page protection and persistence

**Approximate time:** 6:00–6:30

**Screen actions:**

1. On a page with no room left, click **+ Line item** so the refusal message appears, and hold on
   it long enough to be read. The message names the shortfall in pixels and the section that is
   actually holding the height — see the wording note below.
2. Leave the quote.
3. Reopen it from All Quotes.
4. Show that artwork, specifications, side-view geometry, notes, and pages remain restored.

**Voice-over:**

> The system also monitors the printable page area.
>
> If content reaches the available limit, the editor refuses the addition rather than letting it
> disappear past the edge of the sheet. And it doesn’t simply say the page is full—it tells the
> estimator how much room is missing and which section is taking up the height. Something that
> looks acceptable on a large browser canvas should not be cut off in the final PDF.
>
> Changes are saved with the quote, including artwork placement, side-view positioning,
> specifications, notes, and the multi-page structure.
>
> I’ll leave the quote now and reopen it. And here we have the same configuration and layout
> restored. The estimator can continue from where they stopped instead of recreating the page.

**On-screen captions:**

> Refused before it falls off the page  
> Editing progress preserved

**Wording note:** the on-screen message is not "Page is full". It reads, for example:

> No room for a line item — the sheet is 14px short. The blank area inside SPECIFICATIONS is not
> free — the SIDE VIEW column is what sets that height. Shrink the side view (or remove it) to get
> the room.

Captions and narration must not promise different words than the ones the viewer can read.

## Scene 10 — Export and conclusion

**Approximate time:** 6:30–7:00

**Screen actions:**

1. Download the proposal as PNG.
2. Download it as PDF.
3. Open the PDF briefly beside the browser preview.
4. Finish on the clean completed proposal.

**Voice-over:**

> Once everything has been reviewed, the proposal can be downloaded as either a PNG or a PDF.
>
> The exported version follows the same layout visible in the browser, including the specifications,
> artwork, side views, notes, pricing, and any additional pages.
>
> So, in practical terms, the platform takes us from secure access and customer information to a
> structured, editable, and customer-ready quote.
>
> It reduces repeated data entry, standardizes the technical content, and keeps artwork,
> specifications, pricing, and proposal editing in one place.
>
> There’s still room for human judgement—there has to be—but the repetitive and error-prone parts
> of the process are handled by the system.
>
> And that’s really the objective: faster quote preparation, more consistent output, and a much
> clearer process for making changes before the proposal reaches the customer.

**Final on-screen caption:**

> Authenticate → Create → Configure → Edit → Export

## AI voice direction

- Target approximately 135–145 words per minute.
- Use a confident but conversational voice—not a cinematic advertisement voice.
- Pause for roughly half a second between short paragraphs and one second between scenes.
- Slow down slightly when explaining business value or connected behavior.
- Move a little faster through routine data entry.
- Do not read captions aloud when the narration already communicates the same point.
- Keep the natural contractions: “we’re,” “don’t,” “that’s,” and “I’ll.”
- Preserve the occasional conversational line, such as “And, honestly…” or “otherwise this becomes
  a training video,” because those variations stop the narration from sounding machine-written.
- If the final recording runs long, remove sentences instead of accelerating the voice.

## Editing checklist

- Remove waiting, loading, and long typing sequences.
- Use simple cuts rather than excessive transitions.
- Keep background music around 8–12% of the voice volume.
- Add a subtle mouse-click highlight.
- Use captions only for outcomes and section transitions, not every spoken sentence.
- Blur or replace any real customer data that appears unexpectedly.
- Verify the final PNG and PDF contain no test credentials, recovery codes, private notes, or
  unrelated browser tabs.
- Watch the final export once with sound and once muted before sharing it.
